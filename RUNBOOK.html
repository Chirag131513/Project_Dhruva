Dhruva Runbook

Six minutes, one slider, one claim: the review queue you already run is filled the wrong way, and filling it correctly is worth 6–8% of what fraud costs you.

Before you walk in

  • If app\dashboard.html exists, just double-click it. To rebuild, run all three in order: export_console.py → measure_gate.py → build_dashboard.py. The middle one is not optional — the build refuses to run without it.
  • Confirm the top-right reads TEST REPLAY · HELD-OUT DATA, not DEV DATA.
  • Drag the capacity slider 1% → 10% once and back. Arrow keys work too.
  • Open git log --oneline in a second window. You will use it.
  • Have results\figures\graph5_triage.png open as a fallback — it carries the entire talk on its own.
  • Know these cold: 7.9% at 2% capacity · ₹405,514 · sorting by risk score loses money at 1–2% · 14.6 µs.

Do not say 28%

That compared my result against a baseline running no review queue at all, which no merchant does. It is retired. The claim is 6–8% against the queue policy a team already has. If a slide, an old doc, or your own memory says 28%, it is stale — and a judge who finds the weaker framing behind the stronger number will not trust the rest.

There is only one demo

The old Streamlit console (app/console.py) has been deleted. It was built around the α slider for conformal — the method that lost — and it had stopped running anyway. If an old note points you at it: it is gone, and app\dashboard.html is the demo.

The questions

Ordered by likelihood. Say the number, not an argument.

Six to eight percent? That’s not much.

It is 6–8% of realised fraud loss for a config change on top of the model you already run, with no retraining, no new labels, and 14 microseconds of latency. And it is measured against the policy you are running today, not against a strawman. I had a 28% number; it compared against a baseline with no queue at all, I decided that was dishonest, and I cut it myself.

Block 12 · RESULTS §0

Your winning method is one line. Where is the contribution?

The measurement, the negative result, and the blindness map — not the method. Nobody had priced queue composition against a cost-optimal threshold under a hard capacity limit on real payments. I did, I showed the two most common policies lose money at realistic capacity, and I showed the sophisticated alternative loses too — after pre-registering the test that could show it. I am not claiming the rule is novel. It is one line.

Block 12 · Block 9 · 10 seeds · p = 0.0020

I already sort my queue by risk score. Isn’t that basically the same thing?

No, and that is the whole result. At 1% capacity sorting by score loses ₹89,329; at 2% it loses ₹15,091. It sends analysts to cases the model is already confident about, where a human adds cost and changes no decision. The cases worth a human are the ones sitting near the cut, not the ones at the top of the ranking.

Block 12 · the score row of the first table

Isn’t this just DAUNT, or the July conformal papers?

I cite all of them first, and I tested DAUNT’s signal as an arm rather than ignoring it. None of those papers measures rupee benefit against a cost-optimal threshold under a finite queue, and none reports a signal losing. My result contradicts theirs: they report the conformal fix winning; on payments with a real capacity limit it doesn’t. My disagree arm is a boosting-stage-spread proxy, not a reimplementation of DAUNT — say that if pressed.

RESULTS §2 prior-art table

Why not just use Vulcan?

This sits on top of Vulcan and never retrains anything. It takes a score and decides what a human should see. I have no evidence about Vulcan and cannot get any — I have never seen its scores. What I can say is that the effect grows as the scorer improves, 0.7× to 1.1× to 8.6× across three base models, and that your own published material describes an alert budget and roughly 175 human review hours a month. The problem exists there. The effect size is an afternoon’s experiment.

Block 11 · n = 3, and two of the three are broken models — say so if pressed. Directional, not proof

Why not just raise the threshold, or block more?

That’s the baseline, and it’s already cost-optimal per transaction. It cannot do better without trading recall against false positives. Escalation escapes that trade-off — at 10% capacity both improve at once, because a human resolves the case instead of the system guessing.

73.4% vs 49.7% recall; 0.86% vs 1.85% FPR

Are your cost constants invented?

Declared, not measured, and stated as such: ₹1,500 chargeback fee, 25% margin, ₹250 goodwill, ₹40 per review, 5% analyst error. All four are swept ±50%. The conformal arm flips sign on three of the four — one of the reasons I dropped it. The winning rule holds its sign on all four, ranging +₹513,576 to +₹1,409,008. The ranking is rebuilt under each cost vector, so that is the rule as deployed, not a frozen ordering.

State the edges before they are asked for. That sweep is one seed, at 10% capacity, and measures my benefit over the no-queue baseline. The 7.9% margin over the best rival queue policy — the actual headline — is not itself swept. The policies all move together under a shared cost vector so a flip is unlikely, but unlikely is not measured.

Block 9 K5 — conformal only. Do not claim a range for band.

Analyst review isn’t free or perfect.

Both are priced in: ₹40 per case plus a 5% analyst error rate, and cases beyond capacity fall back to the threshold rather than vanishing. The queue is static though — no backlog, no fatigue, no time-of-day effects. That’s a stated limitation.

RESULTS §8

Why not retrain?

Chargeback labels arrive weeks late; my split drops a 7-day window for exactly that reason. During the window that matters, retraining isn’t available. My gate needs no labels to fit at all — the cutoff is a quantile of an unlabelled ranking. I designed an oracle-retrain arm and never ran it, so I can’t size the gap.

H5, untested — say so

How do I know your baseline isn’t weak?

I went looking for that myself. Sweeping fixed thresholds, a flat cut at 0.10 beats my per-transaction Bayes baseline by ₹22,709 — 0.6% in my favour. Two things keep it honest: 0.10 was found by sweeping the test set, which my own method never got, so 0.6% is an upper bound; and the escalation effect is 47× larger than the gap. It’s in the write-up. Report it before anyone finds it.

Block 10 · RESULTS §0b

ROC-AUC 0.896 but PR-AUC 0.523?

Same model, same test set. At 3.4% fraud the false-positive axis is dominated by the negative class, so large swings in absolute false positives barely move ROC. I never report accuracy — it would read 96.6% for a model that approves everything.

Block 1

Does it generalise?

Not below ~0.1% prevalence. On ULB it loses at every setting and fraud coverage is unreportable at all eleven cells — about 40 fraud rows in calibration against a floor of 100. That’s in the write-up as a failed external validation, not a footnote. One dataset carries the positive result.

Block 7 · RESULTS §5

How would you know it was working in production?

You couldn’t, fully — and that is the most important sentence in this submission. A blocked transaction never acquires an outcome. You never learn whether it would have been fraud, so its correct label is unobservable by construction. That is the selective-labels problem, it is §8 in the write-up, and it is why the honest framing of every number here is that these are offline measurements on a replay.

The review queue is the one mechanism that generates outcomes for cases the model wanted to reject — so it is a partial window into the blocked region. But it reaches the cases near the cut, never the confidently-blocked ones. It also cuts backwards: IEEE-CIS’s own labels are the output of somebody else’s prior decision system, so the fraud I can measure is the fraud that system let through.

RESULTS §8 · say this before a judge does — unprompted, it is worth more than any number in the deck

Did you tune anything to get this?

The protocol was frozen and hashed before results existed; results/protocol.lock holds the hash and blocks refuse to run if a constant moved. The one change is a recorded amendment with its prediction written down beforehand. When a sweep later revealed a better α I did not switch to it, because reading an argmin off a test curve is fitting to the test set. And the winning rule was written as a strawman — I did not tune it, it simply won.

What would have made you abandon this?

Five conditions, written down before the run. Four fired. K1 was the one that would have killed the economics; it held. K3 killed conformal as the method, and I changed the project rather than the test.

Two hypotheses refuted. One of my own headlines retracted after I found it was scoring itself on the cells it had calibrated on.
